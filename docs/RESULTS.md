# Results

Draft Results section. Every number is traceable to a file under `results/`.
Tables are generated by `06_evaluation/06_build_manuscript_tables.R` and
`06_evaluation/07_error_location.R`; figures by
`07_figures/10_manuscript_figures.R`.

**Evaluation protocol.** All accuracy figures refer to the waste class on the
spatially clean held-out partition: the 216 of 222 validation tiles that share
no footprint with any development tile. Label 99 is a no-data fill and is
excluded from the loss and from every metric. Models are trained on the 639
development tiles assigned to training by the seed-42 spatial block split (250 m
blocks on tile centroids); the remaining 165 development tiles form the
validation split. Results are means over three seeds, with the standard
deviation in parentheses, under the argmax decision rule.

**Model selection.** Configurations are compared on the validation split and
only then evaluated on the held-out partition. Where a single model is required,
it is chosen by the one-standard-error rule: the highest validation F1, then,
among configurations whose validation F1 lies within one standard error of it,
the lowest deployability gap. Applied to the 54 configurations reported here the
rule is decisive at the first step, selecting the DINOv3 ViT-L encoder
pretrained on SAT-493M and fine-tuned end to end (validation F1 0.822, standard
error 0.004). No quantity derived from the held-out partition enters the choice.

---

## 1. The target in relation to the observation

Three quantities fix what the results that follow can mean.

The imagery has a ground sample distance of 3.81 cm, so a waste pile a metre
across occupies of the order of 700 pixels. The target is large with respect to
the observation: it is resolved many times over, and the mapping problem is not
one of detecting objects smaller than the pixel.

The target is, however, extremely rare. Waste occupies **5.42%** of assessed
pixels in the validation split and **3.30%** in the held-out partition, but
only **0.045%** of the full orthomosaic. The annotated tiles are enriched
roughly **73-fold** relative to the landscape the map is intended to cover,
because annotation proceeded by sampling around known waste locations.

This enrichment is not a defect of the sampling. For a target at 0.045% it is
the only practical way to assemble a training set; a design-based sample would
spend almost all of its effort on background. But it means that accuracy
estimated on the annotated tiles and accuracy obtained on the landscape are
different quantities, measured on different populations. Sections 2 and 3 report
the first. Section 4 projects to the second, and the distance between them is
the central result of this work.

## 2. Accuracy of the three mapping approaches

**[Table I, Fig. 1]** Object-based classification with automated segmentation
reaches F1 **0.447** (0.007). A U-Net trained from scratch reaches **0.693**
(0.043), and the selected pretrained-backbone model reaches **0.736** (0.020).

The deficit of object-based classification is **0.289 F1** against the selected
model, and it arises in the segmentation step rather than in the classifier.
The evidence is direct. Evaluated at segment level, where the classifier is
asked only to label segments that already exist, the same random forest on the
same features reaches F1 **0.818** **[Table II]**; evaluated at pixel level on
the same imagery it reaches 0.447. The classifier is not the limitation. The
partition of the image into objects is: a pile that the segmentation fails to
delineate cannot be recovered by any subsequent decision, whereas semantic
segmentation makes a decision at every pixel and is not constrained in this way.

Segment-level and pixel-level accuracy are different quantities, and Table II
is reported only to document how the GeoBIA classifier was chosen. A
segment-level score measures how well segments are labelled once they exist; it
does not measure how well the resulting map delineates waste on the ground.

A further object-based condition was run in which segment boundaries were
obtained by vectorising the reference mask. It is excluded from every result
reported here: polygons cut from the reference cannot generate a false positive,
so the condition returns precision of exactly 1.000 and measures nothing about
the method. It is retained in `results/02_geobia_pixel/geobia_oracle_rf/` as the
record of that check.

## 3. What pretraining contributes

### 3.1 Pretraining substitutes for annotation

**[Table III, Fig. 2]** The contribution of pretraining is strongly conditional
on how many tiles are labelled.

At **64** training tiles the best pretrained encoder reaches F1 **0.701**
against **0.560** for the same architecture trained from scratch, a gain of
**0.141**. The scratch model does not reach that level until **639** tiles. At
this operating point pretraining is worth approximately a **tenfold reduction
in annotation effort**.

The advantage narrows as labels accumulate. The spread across pretraining
corpora falls from **0.141** at n = 64 to **0.061** at n = 639, and at the full
budget the ordering among corpora is no longer stable across seeds. Pretraining
is best understood here as a substitute for labelled data rather than as a
source of accuracy in its own right.

The same tile subset is drawn for every seed at a given budget, so the spread
across seeds in Table III reflects training stochasticity and not the variance
of the subset itself. The tenfold figure is accordingly an order of magnitude
rather than a point estimate.

### 3.2 Pretraining scale matters more than pretraining domain

**[Table IV]** Among backbones evaluated at the full budget, web-image
pretraining (DINOv3 ViT-L, held-out F1 0.741) and sub-metre aerial pretraining
(SatlasPretrain, 0.738) are indistinguishable from 10 m satellite pretraining
(DINOv3 SAT-493M, 0.736), and all three exceed ImageNet pretraining of a
smaller encoder (EfficientNet-B4, 0.708).

The expectation that a remote sensing corpus should transfer best to remote
sensing data is not supported at this resolution. At 3.81 cm a waste pile is an
object a few hundred pixels across, closer in scale to the objects of a
photographic corpus than to the land-cover parcels of a 10 m satellite corpus.
The comparison is partly confounded: SatlasPretrain uses a Swin-v2-B backbone
whereas the DINOv3 variants are ViT-L/16, so corpus and architecture are not
fully separable.

### 3.3 Fine-tuning outperforms frozen encoders

**[Fig. 3]** Fine-tuned encoders average F1 **0.725** against **0.696** frozen,
a mean gain of **0.029**, with a slightly lower false-positive rate (0.0100
against 0.0106). The direction is consistent across all five backbones.
Training cost is modest either way, at 5.2 to 12.0 minutes per seed on one GPU.

All ten configurations transferred **100% of their checkpoint tensors**,
verified against a freshly initialised model of the same specification and
recorded in `results/06_pretrained_backbones/preflight_report.json`. No reported
configuration silently fell back to random initialisation.

### 3.4 The ranking among the leading models is not stable

The selected model ranks **sixth of 54** on held-out F1, and the held-out
maximum (0.749, DINOv3-SAT at 320 tiles) exceeds it by 0.013 — less than the
seed-to-seed standard deviation of either model. The leading six configurations
span 0.736 to 0.749, a range smaller than the uncertainty attached to any one
of them.

Validation-based and held-out-based rankings therefore disagree, and the
disagreement is itself a finding: with three seeds and this sample size the
leading configurations are not separable. Reporting the held-out maximum as
though it had been chosen in advance would overstate what the comparison
supports. The consequence for Section 4 is that the choice of model is not a
lever on the result.

## 4. Transfer from the annotated sample to the landscape

### 4.1 The projection

Recall is invariant to prevalence; precision is not. For a model with recall *R*
and false-positive rate *F*, precision at prevalence *p* is

    P = R p / (R p + F (1 - p))

Projecting each model's measured recall and false-positive rate from the
annotated distribution to the landscape rate of 0.045% gives an *implied
precision*. Dividing the achieved false-positive rate by the rate that precision
0.5 would require gives a *deployability gap*, equal to 1.0 for a map usable
without verification and larger otherwise.

**[Fig. 4]** For the selected model the implied precision at landscape
prevalence is **0.030**: approximately thirty-three of every thirty-four
flagged pixels would be wrong. The same model would require a prevalence of
about **1.4%**, some thirty times the landscape rate, to reach precision 0.5.

### 4.2 The attainable operating set

Across the 48 configurations that retain at least 35% recall the gap ranges from
**16.0** to **74.6**. None approaches 1.0.

**[Table V, Fig. 5]** The complete set of non-dominated operating points — those
for which no other configuration attains both a higher recall and a lower gap —
contains nine configurations and spans recall 0.058 to 0.807 with gaps from
**11.2 to 35.1**. The frontier is the informative object here, because it shows
what trading recall for precision actually buys: at 0.731 recall the best
attainable gap is 25.8, at 0.642 it is 22.8, at 0.342 it is 15.2, and discarding
all but 6% of the waste still leaves a gap of 11.2.

Every intervention tested moves a configuration **along** this frontier rather
than displacing it. Architecture, pretraining corpus, annotation budget,
fine-tuning depth, hard-negative mining and the complementary loss of Section
4.3 all lie on it or inside it, and none reaches within an order of magnitude of
deployability. Object-based classification with automated segmentation lies well
inside it, with a gap of **56.1** at a recall of only 0.367, so the one
structurally different approach tested does not escape the problem either.

### 4.3 Suppressing false positives trades directly against recall

**[Table VI]** Adding a complementary not-waste term to the loss suppresses
false positives as intended: the false-positive rate falls by a factor of 16
between weights 0.1 and 0.5, from 0.0049 to 0.0003. Recall falls with it, from
0.443 to 0.058. Implied precision at landscape prevalence peaks at **0.062** at
weight 0.25, where recall is 0.342. At weights of 1.0 and above at least one
seed returns zero recall and the gap is undefined. The weight selects a position
on the frontier of Table V; it does not provide a way off it.

### 4.4 Where the false detections occur

**[Table VII]** Across the ten configurations for which per-tile confusion
counts were retained, between **62.7% and 94.6%** of false-positive pixels fall
within tiles that do contain waste, with an unweighted mean of 78%. Only the
most heavily suppressed model, which detects almost nothing, falls below that
range. The errors are therefore predominantly confusions with surfaces that lie
close to waste rather than failures on unfamiliar background.

**[Table VIII, Fig. 6]** The reference masks carry nine land-cover classes, so
every false-positive pixel has a true class and the confusion can be named. Two
quantities are needed, because they answer different questions. The *commission
rate* is the fraction of a class predicted waste; it does not depend on how much
of that class the scene contains, so it is the part that would transfer to
another site. The *share of errors* is the fraction of all false positives
belonging to a class; it depends on class area, and it determines where
verification effort is actually spent here.

The two orderings are almost opposite. Rocky natural surface is
**0.54%** of the assessed scene, yet **18.2%** of its pixels are predicted
waste. That is the highest commission rate of any class and some thirty times
that of built-up surface, which is physically unsurprising at 3.81 cm, where a
rubble field and a debris pile differ mainly in colour. It nonetheless accounts
for only **10.5%** of false positives, because there is so little of it. Soil
and vegetation have among the lowest commission rates, **1.7%** and **0.6%**,
but together they are three quarters of the scene and contribute **76.8%** of
all false-positive pixels. Water body sits between the two, at 4.2% commission
and 5.9% of errors.

**[Fig. 7]** The same four classes in context make the distinction visible.
Rock produces a large contiguous commission over a rubble field that carries
only a small reference polygon, while on soil and vegetation the error is a
margin around waste that is otherwise correctly found.

This distinction bears on the obvious remedies. Hard negatives drawn at random
from the wider scene are overwhelmingly soil and vegetation, the classes that
dominate the error budget by extent but are barely confusable; rock, where the
confusion actually lives, would almost never be sampled. Enlarging the training
set that way addresses the symptom rather than the cause. And it is why suppressing false
positives through a complementary loss trades against recall rather than
improving the map: the surfaces being suppressed are the ones that most
resemble the surfaces being sought.

## 5. Robustness to the conditions of acquisition

Every result above is measured on tiles cut from the same orthomosaic as the
training tiles. The spatially blocked split controls for location, but sensor,
flight, illumination and processing chain are held fixed by construction. A
model applied to a second survey meets none of those conditions. This section
perturbs the held-out imagery and re-evaluates the trained checkpoints on it.
The reference masks are never altered, so every value is measured against the
same reference on the same 256 x 256 grid and remains comparable with the
tables above.

### 5.1 Loss of spatial resolution

**[Table IX, Fig. 8]** The held-out imagery was reduced by area averaging and
restored to the evaluation grid by bilinear interpolation, simulating a coarser
ground sample distance.

At twice the native pixel size, an effective 7.6 cm, the model trained from
scratch retains **65%** of its native accuracy (F1 0.716 to 0.462). It retains
**3%** at 11.4 cm and nothing beyond. Every pretrained encoder tested retains at
least **90%** at 7.6 cm, and the selected model retains **98%** (0.734 to
0.721). At 11.4 cm the selected model still returns F1 0.62, against 0.02 for
the scratch model.

**[Fig. 9]** The failure is visible in the imagery: at 7.6 cm the scratch model
retains only the core of a pile and loses its margins, and by 15.2 cm it returns
almost nothing, while the pretrained encoder still delineates the same object.

The separation is invisible at native resolution. There the same twelve
configurations span 0.678 to 0.741, a range of 0.063 that Section 3.4 showed to
be smaller than the uncertainty on any one of them. One halving of resolution
separates them by a factor of thirty.

### 5.2 Change of scene brightness

**[Table X, Fig. 10]** The held-out imagery was multiplied by a gain and clipped
to the 8-bit range, simulating the exposure and illumination differences between
flights.

The DINOv3 encoders are almost unaffected, retaining **95% to 101%** of native
accuracy across the full range from 0.60 to 1.50. The scratch model retains
**45%** at a gain of 0.60, and the ImageNet-pretrained convolutional encoders
between 51% and 78%. Brightness is the gentler of the two axes for every model,
but it separates them in the same order.

### 5.3 Fine-tuning buys accuracy and costs transportability

The frozen and fine-tuned encoders of Table IV are indistinguishable in Section
5.1 at mild degradation and diverge sharply under strong degradation. For the
DINOv3 ViT-L pretrained on SAT-493M, the fine-tuned encoder retains 98% at
7.6 cm but **44%** at 15.2 cm; the frozen encoder retains 91% at 7.6 cm and
**69%** at 15.2 cm, and still 52% at 30.5 cm. For the web-pretrained ViT-L the
figures at 15.2 cm are 35% fine-tuned against **78%** frozen.

Fine-tuning adapts the representation to the resolution and radiometry of the
survey it was trained on. That is worth 0.029 F1 on that survey (Section 3.3)
and roughly half the retained accuracy on a degraded one. The two findings are
not in conflict; they measure different things.

### 5.4 What this implies

Section 7 records that the configurations converge into a narrow accuracy band
at native resolution, which is consistent with a limit set by the input rather
than by the models. Section 5.1 shows that the convergence is an artefact of
evaluating every model on the imagery it was trained on. Under a shift in
acquisition conditions the same models separate by more than an order of
magnitude.

Accuracy on a single survey is therefore not the quantity that distinguishes
these approaches, and it is not the quantity a second deployment would depend
on. This does not alter the deployability gap of Section 4, which is measured
on the training survey and would only widen on a degraded one. Pretraining buys
transportability, not precision at landscape prevalence.

## 6. Synthesis

1. **Semantic segmentation outperforms object-based classification by 0.289 F1**,
   and the difference is attributable to the segmentation step rather than to the
   classifier: the same classifier reaches 0.818 when segments are given and
   0.447 when it must rely on segments it produced.
2. **Pretraining reduces the annotation requirement by approximately an order of
   magnitude** when labels are scarce, with the advantage concentrated below
   roughly 100 tiles and largely absent at 639. Pretraining scale matters more
   than pretraining domain at centimetre resolution, and fine-tuning is worth
   0.029 F1 over a frozen encoder.
3. **No configuration produces a directly usable map at landscape prevalence.**
   The attainable frontier runs from a gap of 11.2 to 35.1, and its lower end is
   reached only by discarding more than nine tenths of the waste.

The first two findings concern *label scarcity*; the third concerns *target
rarity*. These are distinct problems, and the results indicate that progress on
the first does not transfer to the second. The interventions that buy a tenfold
reduction in annotation effort move the operating point along the frontier of
Table V by a factor of two or three, against a shortfall of one and a half
orders of magnitude. For rare environmental features it is target rarity that
determines whether a map can be used without manual verification, which is why
the inventory reported here required systematic visual checking of candidate
detections.

## 7. Basis and limits of the inference

**The prevalence estimate is model-based, not design-based.** The landscape rate
of 0.045% underpins every implied precision and every gap reported above, and it
is derived from the mapped inventory rather than from a probability sample. It
therefore carries no confidence interval, and the gaps of Table V inherit that
limitation. A stratified accuracy assessment with design-based estimation would
place an interval on the prevalence and, through it, on the gap. This is the
single most valuable addition that could be made to the present analysis.

**The input channel may be saturated.** All configurations operate on
three-channel 8-bit RGB, and the ten backbone configurations of Table IV
converge into a band of held-out F1 from 0.678 to 0.741 despite a wide range of
encoder capacity. That convergence is consistent with a limit set by the
information in the input rather than by the models. The defensible claim is the
conditional one: *no RGB-derived model tested closes the deployability gap*.
Establishing whether the limit is informational would require observations not
used here — multispectral, thermal or radar, or greater radiometric depth. The
28-band tiles available for this site do not serve that purpose, since every
band is a deterministic function of the same RGB orthomosaic (colour indices and
Haralick texture) and adds no independent observation.

**Run-to-run variation exceeds several of the effects compared.** The scratch
model returned 0.704, 0.730 and 0.646 on its three seeds, a range of 0.084.
That range is wider than the entire 0.013 spread of the six leading pretrained
configurations, so a single unreplicated run of it could be reported anywhere
across most of the accuracy scale. Every comparison in this section is made on
three-seed means for that reason, and quantities derived from the
false-positive rate should be read as ranges rather than point estimates.

**External validity is untested.** All results come from one settlement, one
sensor and one acquisition date. Nothing here establishes that the frontier of
Table V occupies the same position at another site, and the prevalence that
drives the gap is site-specific by construction.
