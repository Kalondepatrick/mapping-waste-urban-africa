## The question

> Under what conditions do foundation models improve the generalisability of
> environmental monitoring in data-scarce settings?

## The answer

**Foundation models improve generalisability when labels are scarce. They do not
improve it when the target is rare.** Those are two different kinds of
"data-scarce", and the literature conflates them.

| axis | question | effect of pretraining |
|---|---|---|
| sample efficiency | how few labels to reach accuracy *X*? | **~10× better** |
| deployability | does the map work at real prevalence? | **no measurable effect** |

Standard benchmarks evaluate at development prevalence, where the first axis is
visible and the second is invisible by construction. That is why the field sees
steady progress while the maps stay unusable for rare targets.

## Where pretraining helps

Source: `results/04_label_budget_factorial/label_budget_curve.csv`.

At a 64-tile label budget, DINOv3 reaches F1 **0.7011** against a scratch
U-Net's **0.5603** — +0.14 absolute, a 25 % relative gain. DINOv3 with **64**
tiles matches the scratch U-Net with **639**: a tenfold reduction in annotation
effort, which is the binding constraint for a field project.

The advantage is conditional:

- **Below ~100 labelled tiles** the gain is large.
- **Above ~320 tiles** it vanishes; at the full budget, aerial pretraining versus
  ImageNet on identical architecture is a statistical wash.
- **Scale matters more than domain.** Sub-metre aerial pretraining (Satlas) beat
  object-scale web imagery (DINOv3), which beat 10 m satellite (DINOv3-SAT), on
  3.81 cm drone data. "Use a remote-sensing foundation model because this is
  remote sensing" is the wrong rule at this GSD — match the *scale*.
  Caveat: Satlas is a different architecture (Swin-v2-B), so that comparison is
  confounded with architecture.
- **Fine-tuned beats frozen** consistently, by 0.018–0.034 F1.

### The deployability gap

Precision is not prevalence-invariant; recall is:

```
precision = R·p / (R·p + F·(1 − p))
```

The held-out test partition has a waste prevalence of **3.30 %** (the validation
split, 5.42 %). Landscape prevalence is **0.045 %**, so the test partition is 73×
enriched and the validation split 120×. *Implied precision* projects each model's
recall and FPR to landscape prevalence; *gap* is the achieved FPR divided by the
FPR that would be needed for precision 0.5 there.

**54 configurations are reported**: 53 deep-learning configurations plus GeoBIA
with automated OTB segmentation. A GeoBIA condition using polygons vectorised
from the reference mask was also run but is **excluded from every table and
figure** — such polygons cannot produce a false positive, so the condition
measures nothing about the method. Six further configurations are excluded
because recall collapsed and the gap is undefined or they fall below recall
0.35. Across the remaining **48 usable configurations the gap ranges 16× to
75×**:

| family | configs | gap range |
|---|---|---|
| PyTorch baseline incl. hard negatives | 8 | 16.0–32.7× |
| Pretrained / foundation backbones | 10 | **25.8–38.4×** |
| Label budget factorial | 28 | 22.8–74.6× |
| GeoBIA, automated segmentation | 1 | **56.1×** |

The gap is **not invariant** — it varies more than fourfold, and gets worse at
small label budgets. What is invariant is that nothing approaches 1.0. The
minimum, 16.0×, belongs to a hard-negative variant whose recall is 0.43, so it is
bought by discarding more than half the waste. Among models with competitive
recall the floor is about 23×, and among the foundation backbones 25.8×.

Object-based classification does not escape it either: GeoBIA with automated
segmentation has the **worst gap of any configuration, 56.1×**. The one approach
structurally unlike the others fails the same way.

Decomposition of the shortfall (`06_evaluation/03_prevalence_decomposition.py`):
**base-rate effect 10.4×, confuser shift 3.3×**. 75–82 % of false-positive area
falls inside tiles that do contain waste.

### Hard negatives trade recall for FPR, not for deployability

Source: `results/05_negative_weight_sweep/frontier.csv`. Raising the
complementary not-waste loss weight cuts FPR by up to 27×, but recall collapses
with it:

| weight | F1 | precision | recall | FPR | gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 0.712 | 0.719 | 0.706 | 0.00946 | 29.7× |
| 0.1 | 0.558 | 0.755 | 0.443 | 0.00490 | 24.6× |
| 0.25 | 0.483 | 0.833 | 0.342 | 0.00235 | 15.3× |
| 0.5 | 0.097 | 0.814 | 0.058 | 0.00030 | 11.6× |

The frontier tops out at 0.099 implied precision, and at 0.039 once recall is
held above 0.35. Pretraining *plus* hard negatives buys roughly 1.8× — real, but
an order of magnitude short of what deployment needs.

## Proposed claim

> Foundation models reduce the labelling required for environmental monitoring by
> an order of magnitude, but do not improve performance at deployment prevalence;
> for rare targets, only the latter determines whether a map is usable.

## Boundary of the claim

All 53 configurations used RGB. They converged into a seven-point F1 band
(0.678–0.741) across a 450-fold range of model capacity — the signature of a
saturated input channel. The honest statement is that **no RGB model** closes the
gap, and the limit looks informational rather than algorithmic.

Two things would settle it, neither done:

1. **A different sensor.** Nothing in `data/` can settle this. The 28-band tiles
   in `data/tiled_data/multiband/` are RGB plus colour indices (SAVI, VARI) and
   Haralick texture (variance, entropy, dissimilarity), all computed from the
   same orthomosaic for the GeoBIA stage. They are deterministic functions of the
   three channels the networks already receive, so they carry no new information
   and **cannot test the informational ceiling**. Settling it needs multispectral,
   thermal or SAR data, or greater radiometric depth than 8-bit.

   Those tiles do support a narrower question worth asking: whether
   hand-engineered texture is a useful inductive bias at small label budgets,
   where a network may not learn it from 64 tiles even though its receptive field
   could represent it. That belongs on the label-budget axis, not here.
2. **A stratified accuracy sample** over the full landscape. Every prevalence
   figure above rests on the estimate of landscape prevalence; a design-based
   sample would put a confidence interval on it.

A third, smaller item: the 28-configuration factorial drew its label-budget
subsets once rather than per seed, so its small-*n* error bars capture
initialisation variance only. `--resample-budget-per-seed` is implemented but
the factorial has not been rerun with it. Treat the *n* = 64 and *n* = 128 points
as provisional.
